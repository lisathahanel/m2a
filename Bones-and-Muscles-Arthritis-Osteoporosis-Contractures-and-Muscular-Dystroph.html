Today we’re going to walk through four important conditions that affect the bones and muscles. I’m going to talk you through each one step by step, just as if we’re in the classroom together. Don’t worry if some of these terms sound complicated at first — by the end of this lesson you’ll understand what each condition really means, why it happens, and what it looks like in real life.

1. Arthritis – When Joints Become Inflamed and Painful

Let’s start with arthritis. The word itself simply means “joint inflammation.” When someone has arthritis, the joints — the places where two bones meet — become swollen, stiff, and painful. There are two main types we need to understand: osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.

Osteoarthritis – The “Wear and Tear” Type

Imagine a door hinge that has been opened and closed thousands of times. Eventually the metal wears down and the door starts to stick. Osteoarthritis works in a similar way. Over many years the smooth cartilage that cushions the ends of the bones slowly breaks down. Without that cushion, the bones begin to rub against each other. That rubbing causes stiffness, pain, and sometimes swelling.

This is by far the most common form of arthritis. In fact, about 90 percent of people over the age of 50 have some degree of osteoarthritis. It very often shows up in the fingers, but it can also affect the knees, hips, and spine. The good news is that anti-inflammatory medicines can help reduce the swelling and ease the pain.

Rheumatoid Arthritis – The Auto-Immune Type

Rheumatoid arthritis is different and usually more serious. In this condition the body’s own immune system becomes confused and starts attacking the joints as if they were foreign invaders. The result is not only pain and swelling, but also gradual deformity of the joints. In some people it can even affect organs such as the heart, lungs, and muscles.

Because rheumatoid arthritis is an auto-immune disease, it is harder to treat than ordinary osteoarthritis. Doctors often need stronger medicines that calm down the immune system itself.

2. Osteoporosis – When Bones Become Fragile

Now let’s move to the bones themselves. Osteoporosis means “porous bones.” Calcium is the mineral that makes bones strong and dense. When the body loses too much calcium, the bones become full of tiny holes — like a sponge instead of solid wood. These weak bones break much more easily.

Who is most at risk? People who have a family history of osteoporosis, those who do not get enough calcium in their diet, and people who are not very active. Low activity is especially important because bones actually become stronger when we use them.

One of the visible signs of osteoporosis is a gradual loss of height. The bones in the spine (the vertebrae) can slowly collapse, causing the back to curve forward. This makes movement painful and reduces the range of motion.

Treatment focuses on rebuilding strength. Doctors recommend increasing calcium through dairy foods or supplements, and they often encourage weight-bearing exercise or light weight training. When muscles pull on the bones, it helps the body absorb and keep calcium where it is needed.

3. Contractures – When Muscles and Tissues Tighten Permanently

Next we come to contractures. A contracture happens when muscles, skin, tendons, or ligaments shorten and tighten so much that the joint can no longer move through its normal range. The limb may become stuck in a bent or twisted position.

What causes this? The three most common reasons are:

  • Staying in bed for a long time without moving,
  • Scarring after a serious injury (especially burns), and
  • Simply not using a joint for a long period (immobilization).

As care workers, you play a very important role here. You may be asked to help with range-of-motion exercises and regular repositioning. Moving the joints gently every day keeps the tissues flexible and improves circulation. This is one of the main reasons we reposition residents every two hours — it helps prevent contractures from developing in the first place.

4. Muscular Dystrophy – Progressive Muscle Weakness

Finally, let’s talk about muscular dystrophy, often shortened to MD. This is an inherited condition caused by a genetic defect. Healthy skeletal muscles gradually become weaker and weaker over time. There is currently no cure and no way to stop the disease from progressing. It is not contagious — you cannot catch it from someone who has it.

There are several different types of muscular dystrophy. Today we will look at the three most common ones.

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy

This type usually appears early in childhood, between the ages of 2 and 6. Children may start to walk with a waddling gait or walk on their toes. They often have trouble running and may develop a protruding abdomen. Duchenne muscular dystrophy is much more common in boys than in girls.

Facioscapulohumeral Muscular Dystrophy

This form usually becomes noticeable in the teenage years. The name is long, but it simply tells us which muscles are affected first: the face (facio), the shoulder blades (scapulo), and the upper arms (humeral). Facial weakness is almost always present — the person may have difficulty closing their eyes tightly or whistling. Other signs include trouble raising the arms, difficulty lifting objects, and shoulder blades that stick out prominently.

Myotonic Muscular Dystrophy

Myotonic dystrophy usually appears in early adulthood, though it can sometimes show up in the teenage years. The key feature is myotonia — the muscles have trouble relaxing after they contract. A person may grip something and then find it hard to let go. The hands and feet often feel stiff, especially after being cold, and there is usually some facial weakness as well.

So, let’s quickly recap what we have covered today:

  • Arthritis is inflammation of the joints — osteoarthritis from wear and tear, rheumatoid from the immune system attacking the body.
  • Osteoporosis is loss of bone density that makes bones brittle and more likely to break.
  • Contractures are permanent shortenings of muscles and soft tissues that limit movement.
  • Muscular dystrophy is a group of inherited diseases that cause progressive muscle weakness.
